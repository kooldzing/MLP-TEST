What On-Chain Actually Means

Decentralisation is not a badge a project either holds or lacks. It is a set of separate dials, and most of them are set by hand.

19 Aug 2026 5 min read

On-chain has drifted into use as a synonym for trustworthy, which is roughly like using stainless steel as a synonym for safe. It describes a material property, not a guarantee about the object you are holding.

The precise meaning is narrow. A piece of state is on-chain if it lives in the replicated ledger that validators maintain, and if anyone can independently recompute it from the network's history and arrive at the same answer. The valuable property is not that it sits on a blockchain. It is that you do not have to take a particular company's word for it. Apply that definition strictly and a surprising amount of what gets called on-chain turns out to be somewhere else entirely.

The parts that are usually elsewhere

  • The image. A token's picture is normally a link inside a metadata file, and that file is often itself a link. The ledger faithfully records the pointer. It does not record the thing, and links rot.
  • The interface. The site you use is a domain name, a registrar, a hosting provider and a build pipeline. Domains get hijacked and front ends get swapped for hostile copies, and the chain underneath notices nothing unusual.
  • The reading. Almost nobody runs their own node. Your wallet asks an RPC provider what your balance is and displays the reply. That reply is verifiable in principle and unverified in practice.
  • The prices. Anything that references an external value depends on an oracle, which is a set of parties reporting off-chain information and being trusted, to varying degrees, to report it honestly.
  • The decisions. Governance frequently resolves to a multisignature wallet held by a small group. Better than one person, and not the same as nobody being in charge.

The upgrade authority question

This is the item that matters most and gets asked least. On Solana a program is an account that holds executable code, and it can be deployed with an upgrade authority, meaning an address permitted to replace that code with entirely different code. Same address, same name in your wallet, new behaviour, no migration and no announcement required.

This is not inherently sinister. It is how bugs get fixed and how young software survives contact with real users. But the sentence the contract is on-chain, so it cannot be changed is simply false unless that authority has been revoked, which is a public and checkable fact and frequently has not happened. A program that a single key can rewrite is, in every way that matters to you, a service operated by whoever holds that key.

A program that one key can rewrite is not a law of physics. It is a service, and it has a landlord.

Decentralisation is several dials

Treating it as a binary is what produces the arguments. It is more useful to ask a handful of separate questions, because a system can score well on some and badly on others.

  • Who is allowed to write? Can anyone submit a transaction, or is there an allowlist somewhere in the path?
  • Who can verify without permission, and what does independent verification actually cost in hardware and bandwidth?
  • Who can censor? Can a leader or a sequencer decline to include your transaction, and if so, what recourse exists and how long does it take?
  • Who can change the rules? Upgrade keys, governance votes, and the client development teams whose code everyone runs.
  • Who can stop it? Halting and restarting a network requires coordination, and any group capable of coordinating that is a group with power over the system.

The answers differ by network, by layer and by month. A chain becomes more decentralised as independent client implementations mature and less so as stake concentrates among a few operators. It is a measurement that moves, not a label that gets awarded once.

Why the distinction is practical

Because failure follows dependencies. A large share of incidents reported as hacks were nothing of the sort at the ledger level. A domain was hijacked, or a front end was replaced, or a signing key was phished, or an oracle reported something absurd and the code obediently believed it. The chain executed correctly. A human authority sitting beside it was the weak point, and that authority was usually visible in advance to anyone who thought to look.

The most useful reflex is a single question, asked before any of the technical ones. If everyone involved in building this disappeared tomorrow, what would keep working, and for how long? The parts that survive that question are the parts that were genuinely on-chain. Everything else was a service wearing the word as a costume.
