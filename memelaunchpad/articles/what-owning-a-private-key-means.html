What Owning a Private Key Actually Means

There are no coins in your wallet. There is a number that proves you may move entries in a ledger, and that difference changes everything.

14 Aug 2026 6 min read

Wallet is the worst piece of naming in this industry, and the confusion it creates has probably cost people more than any single exploit. A wallet contains a folded object with your money in it. A crypto wallet contains no assets whatsoever. It contains keys, and a viewer, and a button that signs things.

A number and its shadow

A private key is a very large random number. Solana uses the Ed25519 signature scheme, and from that number a one-way function derives a public key, which is what you hand out as your address. The one-way part is the entire foundation. Deriving the address from the key takes microseconds. Deriving the key from the address is not something anyone can do, which is a statement about mathematics rather than about how hard someone is trying.

Signing works like this. You take a message, in practice a transaction, and combine it with the private key to produce a signature. Anyone can check that signature against your public key and confirm two things at once: that the holder of the key authorised something, and that the something was exactly this message and not a slightly different one. The key itself never travels. That verification, repeated by every validator, is the whole security model.

Ownership therefore means something quite specific. There is no vault. There is a ledger, and the network accepts an instruction to move a balance only if it carries a valid signature from the address holding it.

This is why sharing your address is harmless and why a photograph of a seed phrase is a bearer instrument. One is the lock. The other is the key, and it can be copied without being taken.

Twelve words are not a password

A seed phrase is a human-readable encoding of the random entropy from which keys are generated, using a standard wordlist so that transcription errors are detectable. A defined derivation path turns that single seed into an unlimited tree of keypairs, which is why restoring one phrase restores every account you ever created in that wallet.

The important consequence is that the phrase is not a password guarding an account held somewhere else. It is the account. There is no reset, no support desk and no reversal, because there is no party with the authority to grant one. A backup that exists in exactly one place is one fire away from gone. A backup sitting in a cloud folder is one reused password away from gone. Both failure modes are extremely common and neither involves anybody breaking cryptography.

What an exchange is instead

On a centralised exchange you do not have keys. You have a row in a company's database and a promise attached to it. The exchange holds keys to pooled wallets, your balance is an internal number, and your trades are matched in their engine without touching a chain at all. Deposits and withdrawals are the only moments the ledger is involved.

This is not automatically worse. It is a different risk with a different shape. You have swapped the possibility of losing your own key for the possibility that the institution is fraudulent, insolvent or badly run, in which case customers have historically discovered that they are unsecured creditors rather than owners. There is also the solvent-but-unavailable case, where an account is frozen for compliance reasons or withdrawals are suspended during exactly the volatility that made you want to move.

Self-custody does not remove risk. It converts counterparty risk into operational risk, and operational risk is entirely yours.

The failure modes move, they do not vanish

  • Loss. A misplaced phrase is final in a way almost nothing else in modern life is final.
  • Theft through the ordinary computer security channels. Malware, fake download pages, clipboard substitution, a browser extension that was sold to someone new last month.
  • Blind signing. A hardware wallet protects the key from your computer. It does not protect your judgement. Approving a transaction whose contents you cannot read is trusting the interface that composed it.
  • Standing permissions. On Solana a token account can have a delegate authorised to move tokens on your behalf, and authorities over accounts can be reassigned. A single signature can grant ongoing access rather than perform a one-off transfer, which is the mechanic behind a great many drained wallets.
  • Interface deception. The chain will faithfully execute a transaction that does something other than what the page said it would.

The middle of the spectrum

The choice is not binary, and treating it as binary is how people end up with everything inside one browser extension. Multisignature programs require several of a set of keys to approve anything, removing the single point of failure without handing control to a company. Keeping long-term holdings on a device that never touches a browser while a small hot wallet handles daily activity is unglamorous and effective. Regulated custody is a reasonable answer for people who want a legally accountable counterparty.

Not your keys, not your coins is true as far as it goes. What the slogan leaves out is the second half of the sentence. Being your own bank also means being your own security team, your own backup policy and your own disaster recovery plan, permanently. The right setup matches the discipline you actually have, rather than the discipline you imagine you would find in a crisis.
